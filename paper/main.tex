\begin{abstract}
Stationarity rewards memory, but after change the same history can mislead.
We ask when forgetting should be permitted.
E-process-authorized Thompson
sampling (\method{}) gives each arm full-history and discounted Beta
states.
An anytime-valid e-process first authorizes the discounted
state, then a reversible relevance score controls its influence.
Before authorization, \method{} exactly follows optimistic Thompson sampling
(\anchor{}). Under a Beta--Bernoulli prior-predictive stationary model, \method{}'s
probability of ever departing from \anchor{} is at most the chosen $\alphaE$,
without fitted thresholds.
Relative to \method{}, removing authorization increased mean normalized
dynamic pseudo-regret by $38.4\%$ on the registered suite but reduced it
by $7.5\%$ on the literature-derived replay suite.
Therefore, evidence controls when
adaptation begins, not whether it always helps.
\end{abstract}

\section{When should a bandit stop trusting its history?}

In a non-stationary bandit, history can become misleading. Existing methods
discount or window past observations, adapt their forgetting rate, or restart
learning on a schedule
\citep{garivier2008ucb,raj2017taming,lu2017adaptive,besbes2014rexp3,
russac2019weighted,fiandri2024swts}. Detector-based methods instead wait for
evidence of change \citep{cao2018mucb,liu2017cusum,besson2022glr}. We ask
whether such evidence can also control the probability that a policy leaves a
trusted stationary anchor.

E-processes provide the statistical tool. Under a declared null model,
an e-process crosses $1/\delta$ with probability at most $\delta$, without
fixing a stopping time or horizon
\citep{ville1939,vovk2021evalues,grunwald2024safe,ramdas2023savi}. E-detectors
combine e-processes started at different times to monitor changes at unknown times
\citep{shin2023edetectors}. Mixtures over geometrically spaced candidate
change locations are also established \citep[Section~4.4]{ramdas2022exchangeability}.
We use the resulting e-process threshold crossing to authorize departure.
Our anchor is the Bernoulli form of Optimistic Bayesian
Sampling \citep{thompson1933,may2012optimistic}, which we call optimistic
Thompson sampling (\anchor{}). It uses each arm's full-history Beta posterior.
In \method{}, the discounted posterior
cannot affect an action before authorization, so the crossing bound also bounds
departure from the anchor.
Our contribution is a controller that translates statistical authorization into
prior-predictive control of departure from \anchor{}, sufficient evidence
conditions under adaptive sampling, and an empirical evaluation of
\method, including authorization's environment-dependent cost. When the anchor
is trusted under the declared model, limiting unnecessary departures is valuable.
Conservative bandits constrain reward relative to a baseline \citep{wu2016conservative}.
We constrain departure from the anchor’s trajectory.

\paragraph{Setup.}
There are $K$ arms and $T$ rounds. At round $t$, the policy selects $A_t$. If
this is arm $k$'s $n$th pull, its reward is
$Y_t=X_{k,n}\in\{0,1\}$, and $N_k(t)$ counts its pulls through round $t$. Our
stationary model is
\begin{equation}
 P_k\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathrm{Beta}(1,1),\qquad
 X_{k,n}\mid P_k\stackrel{\mathrm{ind}}{\sim}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(P_k).
 \label{eq:null}
\end{equation}
Each arm draws one probability and keeps it forever. The guarantee averages
over these draws and does not hold separately for every fixed set of arm
probabilities.

\section{From evidence to action}
\label{sec:method}

We use this stationary model to decide when the policy may supplement its
full-history anchor with short-memory estimates.
Each arm maintains two Beta states initialized at $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$:
a full-history posterior that retains every reward from that arm, and a
discounted short-memory pseudo-posterior.
Each round, the short-memory counts above the prior are multiplied by
$\gamma_S=0.995$ before the played arm incorporates its reward,
giving an effective memory of about 200 rounds.
Evidence grants arm-specific authorization for the short-memory state
to affect action selection, relevance measures continuing disagreement,
and a decision weight determines its influence.

\paragraph{Has the arm produced evidence against stationarity?}
To decide whether arm $k$ can use its short-memory posterior, we construct an
arm-specific e-process that compares the declared stationary model with
models allowing for exactly one change at an unknown time.

The change time is unknown, so we consider several candidate change-points. Candidate $j$ places a possible change-point at arm-local pull $s_j$. The candidate models the rewards through $s_j - 1$ using one constant Bernoulli probability and models the rewards from pull $s_j$ onward using a second constant probability. The two probabilities may differ.
For a contiguous block of arm-local rewards
$X_{k,a:b}=(X_{k,a},\ldots,X_{k,b})$, let
$S_{a:b}=\sum_{\ell=a}^b X_{k,\ell}$ and
$F_{a:b}=b-a+1-S_{a:b}$ be the block's number of successes and failures.
Under a $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$ prior, the Beta--Bernoulli marginal likelihood of the block is
$m(X_{k,a:b})=\mathrm B(1+S_{a:b},1+F_{a:b})$.
The evidence for a split at change-point $s_j$, relative to a
stationary model over all $n$ pulls, is

\begin{equation}
 L_{k,n}^{(j)}=
 \frac{m(X_{k,1:s_j-1})\,m(X_{k,s_j:n})}{m(X_{k,1:n})}.
 \label{eq:ecomponent}
\end{equation}

Every pull of an arm could be a change-point, although this would require tracking $n-1$ candidates after $n$ pulls.
Instead, we use the geometric grid $s_1=2$ and $s_{j+1}=\lceil1.25\,s_j\rceil$.
If a change occurs at pull $s$, the most recent grid point $s_j\le s$ lies less than 20 percent
earlier.
So, every possible change-point has a nearby candidate while
only $O(\log n)$ candidates must be tracked rather than $O(n)$.

Since we do not know which candidate is closest to the actual change-point,
we assign candidate $j$ the fixed weight
$\pi_j=1/[j(j+1)]$ so
$\sum_j\pi_j=1$, and let $J(n)=\#\{j:s_j\le n\}$.
Unreached candidates have $L_{k,n}^{(j)}=1$, and their weights sum to
$1/(J(n)+1)$.
Therefore,

\begin{equation}
 E_{k,n}=\sum_{j=1}^{J(n)}\pi_j\,L_{k,n}^{(j)}
 \;+\;\frac{1}{J(n)+1}.
 \label{eq:monitor}
\end{equation}

The arm-specific evidence process $E_{k,n}$ starts at one and is a nonnegative martingale under \eqref{eq:null}. Arm $k$ becomes authorized when $E_{k,n}$ reaches $K/\alpha_E$, setting the authorization latch to $C^E_{k,t}=\1\{\max_{n\le N_k(t)}E_{k,n}\ge K/\alpha_E\}$.
The maximum keeps $C^E_{k,t}$ at one after its first crossing.

\paragraph{Do recent rewards still disagree with the long-history model?}

The authorization latch records whether the arm's evidence process has ever
crossed its threshold. Since the latch is permanent, it does not indicate
whether recent rewards still disagree with the long-history posterior's predictions. Therefore, we maintain a relevance score for the newly arriving rewards.

Before observing arm $k$'s $n$th reward, we record the long-history
posterior's prediction.
After observing the reward, we compute its residual
from the long-history posterior's prediction and update an exponentially weighted average with a memory of approximately $\sqrt T$ arm-local pulls.
We normalize the magnitude of this average by a corresponding scale, giving the relevance score $r_{k,n}\in[0,1]$.
Values of relevance near zero mean little sustained disagreement, while larger values indicate persistent recent disagreement
with the long-history model. Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm} gives the exact update and normalization.

\paragraph{How strongly should the short-memory posterior influence the choice?}

At round $t$, authorization and current relevance form the gate
\begin{equation}
g_{k,t}=C^E_{k,t-1} \cdot r_{k,N_k(t-1)},\qquad
R_{k,t}=\frac{n^S_{k,t}}{n^S_{k,t}+\rho},\qquad
w_{k,t}=g_{k,t}^{\nu}
+\zeta(1-g_{k,t}^{\nu})g_{k,t}R_{k,t},
\label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}
Here, $n^S_{k,t}$ is the amount of data retained by the short-memory
posterior, $\rho$ sets the retained-data scale, $\nu$ controls how strongly
the weight responds to the gate, and $\zeta$ controls how retained data further
increase the short-memory posterior's influence. Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm} gives their
fixed values.
For each arm, the decision weight $w_{k,t}$ is used to combine the two posterior draws and the two posterior means.
The arm's selection score is the larger of these two weighted combinations, and the policy selects the arm with the largest score.

Thus, authorization determines whether adaptation is permitted, relevance
gauges whether recent disagreement persists, and the decision weight
determines how strongly the policy adapts. Before any arm is authorized,
$g_{k,t}=w_{k,t}=0$ for every arm, so no short-memory draw is used and
\method{} exactly reproduces \anchor{} under the same long-posterior draws and
tie-breaking. Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm} gives the complete algorithm and
update order.


\section{When the policy can depart from its anchor}

We first show that under the declared stationary model, \method{} is unlikely
to receive permission to depart from \anchor{}.
We then give sufficient conditions for an arm's evidence to cross the
authorization threshold when its rewards change.

Let $\tauth$ be the first round where
$C^E_{k,t-1}=1$ for at least one arm, and let $\tdep$ be the first round where \method{} and \anchor{} select different arms.
We couple the policies by starting them from identical states and giving
them the same arm-specific reward sequences, policy-randomness stream,
and tie-breaking rule.

\begin{theorem}[Controlled departure]
\label{thm:main}
Pathwise, $\tdep\ge\tauth$, and before $\tauth$ the two policies have identical
actions, observed rewards, and long-posterior states. Under \eqref{eq:null},
\begin{equation}
 \Pp(\tdep\le T)\le\Pp(\tauth\le T)\le\alphaE.
 \label{eq:departure}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Why does the guarantee hold?}
For each candidate change-point, $L_{k,n}^{(j)}$ is a likelihood-ratio
martingale under the prior-predictive stationary model.
Their fixed weighted average, $E_{k,n}$, is therefore also a nonnegative martingale starting at one.
Adaptive arm selection does not invalidate this property because an arm is
selected before its next reward is revealed.
Ville's inequality bounds each arm's crossing probability by $\alphaE/K$, and a union bound over the $K$ arms bounds the probability of any authorization by $\alphaE$.

Before authorization, every gate and decision weight is zero, so the coupled
policies have the same trajectory. A departure therefore requires an earlier
e-process crossing, turning the preceding statistic bound into
\eqref{eq:departure}. The resulting threshold $K/\alphaE$ requires no
horizon-specific fitting. \qed

The departure bound averages over the $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$ probabilities drawn
in \eqref{eq:null}. It does not hold separately for every fixed set of reward
probabilities, and it certifies permission to depart rather than the regret or
behavior afterward. Appendix~\ref{app:proof} gives the formal filtration and
coupling argument.

\paragraph{When is the evidence sufficient for authorization?}

Here, we ask when an actual change can produce enough evidence to authorize adaptation.
After $n$ pulls, candidate change-point $s_j$ divides the arm's rewards into segments of lengths $n_0=s_j-1$ and $n_1=n-s_j+1$.
Let $\widehat p_0$ and $\widehat p_1$ be their empirical means and $\widehat p$ the pooled mean.
Define
\[
I_{j,n}
=
n_0\kl(\widehat p_0\Vert\widehat p)
+
n_1\kl(\widehat p_1\Vert\widehat p),
\]
where $\kl$ is the Kullback--Leibler divergence between Bernoulli
distributions. $I_{j,n}$ is the gain in maximized log likelihood from
fitting separate reward probabilities before and after $s_j$, instead of one
probability to all $n$ rewards.

Appendix~\ref{app:evidence} proves
\[
\log E_{k,n}
\ge
I_{j,n}
-\log((n_0+1)(n_1+1))
-\log(j(j+1)).
\]
The two penalties come from averaging the segment probabilities and weighting
candidate $j$ by $1/[j(j+1)]$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:adaptive-power} in Appendix~\ref{app:evidence} gives a sufficient
condition for authorization across all candidates and completed observation
counts of one arm, even when earlier rewards determine when that arm is pulled.
Theorem~\ref{thm:finite-power} gives a sharper sufficient condition when the change occurs
at a fixed candidate and both segment lengths are chosen before seeing the rewards.
Because \method{} does not force exploration, neither theorem guarantees that
a changed arm will be sampled enough to satisfy the stated condition
or authorized within a fixed number of rounds.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We report mean normalized dynamic pseudo-regret
$\bar R_T=T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^T(\max_k\mu_{k,t}-\mu_{A_t,t})$, where
$\mu_{k,t}$ is arm $k$'s mean reward. Lower is better. The registered suite
contains 16,380 non-stationary environments across nine mechanisms and 20
choices of $K$ and $T$, plus 1,820 stationary environments. The primary
comparator, the open ramp, removes the authorization latch and lets relevance
act immediately.
Before evaluation, we chose \method{}'s regret minus the open ramp's regret
as the primary contrast and required it to be below $-0.0002$.
The replay suite compares \method{} with 16 baselines across 4,500
matched runs on 606 mean trajectories from nine literature-derived
environment families.
We compare policies only within a suite.
Appendix~\ref{app:experiments} gives the full design and results.

\paragraph{In these stationary experiments, \method{} almost never leaves its anchor.}
Across 1,820 stationary environments, only one arm was authorized in total.
Mean regret was $0.01157$ for \method{} and \anchor{}, versus $0.02937$ for the
open ramp.

\paragraph{Authorization reduced regret on the registered suite and increased it on replay.}
Using the open ramp as the denominator, \method{}'s regret is $27.7\%$ lower on
the registered suite and $8.2\%$ higher on the replay suite. On the raw scale,
subtracting the open ramp's regret from \method{}'s gives $-0.01497$ with a 95\% interval of
$[-0.01533,-0.01461]$ on the registered suite and $+0.01154$ with an
interval of $[0.01089,0.01218]$ on the replay suite.
Appendix~\ref{app:experiments} examines the reversal.

\paragraph{The practical GLR is close, but its guarantee is different.}
We evaluate two alternatives that replace only e-process authorization
with generalized likelihood-ratio (GLR) rules \citep{besson2022glr}.
The certified GLR is valid for every fixed stationary Bernoulli mean,
beyond our prior-predictive null.
The practical GLR has no finite-sample guarantee.
On the registered and replay suites, regret differences relative
to \method{} are $+0.0028,+0.0221$ for the certified GLR
and $+0.0002,-0.00006$ for the practical GLR.
Tuning the GLR separately for 20 geometries
gives $0.0387$ regret versus \method{}’s $0.0390$ on the
registered suite, without an analytic guarantee.
Appendix~\ref{app:glr-comparator} gives details.

\section{What this establishes}

Under the declared model, the probability of departure is at most $\alpha_E$,
and expected normalized pseudo-regret differs from the anchor’s by at
most $\alpha_E$.
The policy forces no exploration and has mean replay rank 9.72,
placing 12th of 17 methods by mean rank and 13th by mean regret.
Future work should develop authorization that is restartable and valid for
fixed means, together with non-stationary regret guarantees.
